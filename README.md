## What is MacroCutie?

MacroCutie is a **free, web-based nutrition calculator designed specifically for people who eat at fast food and chain restaurants in Canada and North America**. It lets you look up the exact calories, protein, carbohydrates, fat, saturated fat, sodium, sugar, fibre, and cholesterol in your meal — before you eat it.

### The problem it solves

Most nutrition tracking apps (MyFitnessPal, Cronometer, etc.) are built around cooking at home: scanning barcodes, logging ingredients, creating custom recipes. They treat restaurant eating as a secondary feature. As a result:

- **Canadian chains are missing or incomplete** — Tim Hortons, Harvey's, Swiss Chalet, Mary Brown's, and A&W Canada are often absent or have outdated data
- **Canadian menu variations aren't accounted for** — some chains have different portion sizes, ingredients, or items in Canada vs the US
- **The experience is clunky** — you shouldn't need to scan a barcode to look up what's in a Whopper

MacroCutie solves this by being entirely focused on eating out at the chains that Canadians and North Americans actually visit every week.

### What makes it different

| Feature | MacroCutie | Typical nutrition apps |
|---|---|---|
| Canadian chain focus | ✅ Tim Hortons, Harvey's, Swiss Chalet, Mary Brown's | ❌ Often missing |
| Canadian menu variants | ✅ Uses Canadian nutrition guides | ❌ Usually US data |
| Condiment tracking | ✅ Brand-accurate (Heinz, Hellmann's, Frank's) | ❌ Generic estimates |
| Location-aware | ✅ 58+ cities, Toronto neighbourhoods | ❌ Not applicable |
| Dietary filters | ✅ Vegan, vegetarian, halal, gluten-free | Varies |
| Free | ✅ Always | Often freemium |

## How to use it

### Step 1 — Set your location 📍
Click the **"Set location"** button on the home page and select your city. The app will immediately show which chains are available near you at the top of the list. If you're in Toronto, you can then pick your neighbourhood (Scarborough, North York, Etobicoke, Downtown, Mississauga, etc.) for even more specific results.

Your location is remembered for next time. You can change or clear it at any time.

### Step 2 — Find a restaurant 🏪
Browse the restaurant list or use the search bar. Apply dietary filters (vegetarian, vegan, halal, gluten-free) to narrow results. Click any restaurant card to open its full menu.

### Step 3 — Build your meal 🍔
Click menu items to add them to your meal tray. You can add multiple items from multiple restaurants — MacroCutie calculates your combined nutrition across everything in your meal. Adjust quantities with the + / − controls.

### Step 4 — Add condiments 🧂
The condiment picker lets you add sauces, toppings, and extras with brand-accurate calorie counts — Heinz ketchup, Hellmann's mayo, Frank's RedHot, and 40+ more, all at precise serving sizes.

### Step 5 — Get your full breakdown 📊
Hit **"Calculate Nutrition"** to see your complete macro breakdown: total calories, protein, carbs, fat, saturated fat, sodium, sugar, fibre, and cholesterol — each shown as a percentage of the recommended daily intake for an average adult on a 2,000-calorie diet.

## Understanding your macros

This section explains, in plain English, every number MacroCutie tracks. You don't need a nutrition degree to use this app — but knowing what these numbers actually mean helps you make better choices.

---

### 🔥 Calories (kcal)

**What it is:** The unit of energy in all food. Calories measure how much energy your body gets from eating something.

**Why it matters:** Your body burns calories constantly — even while sleeping — to power breathing, circulation, organ function, and movement. When you eat more calories than you burn, the excess is stored as body fat. When you eat fewer than you burn, your body draws on stored fat for energy.

**In fast food:** Fast food is calorie-dense. A single "combo meal" (burger + fries + drink) can easily deliver 1,200–1,800 calories — 60–90% of an average adult's full daily needs in one sitting.

**Daily goal:** ~2,000 kcal for an average adult (this varies widely by body size, age, sex, and activity level)

**Fast food context:**
- McDonald's Big Mac: 590 cal
- Large Fries: 490 cal  
- Medium Coke: 290 cal
- That combo alone: **1,370 cal** — 68% of a daily goal before condiments

---

### 💪 Protein (g)

**What it is:** Protein is made of amino acids — the building blocks your body uses for almost everything: repairing and growing muscle tissue, making enzymes and hormones, maintaining your immune system, and transporting nutrients in the blood.

**Why it matters:** Protein is the most satiating (filling) macronutrient — it keeps you fuller for longer compared to carbs or fat. This is why high-protein meals tend to reduce overall calorie intake. For anyone exercising, adequate protein is essential for muscle repair and growth.

**In fast food:** Most fast food has a reasonable amount of protein (20–47g per meal) because the main items are meat-based. However, that protein comes packaged with substantial saturated fat and sodium. Grilled chicken options are the best balance of high protein and lower fat.

**Daily goal:** ~50g per day for an average adult (athletes may need 1.2–2.2g per kg of bodyweight)

**Fast food context:**
- Chipotle Chicken Bowl: 47g ✅
- Tim Hortons Double Double (coffee): 3g — beverages are poor protein sources
- Wendy's Baconator: 61g — very high protein, but also 62g fat

---

### 🍞 Carbohydrates / Carbs (g)

**What it is:** Carbohydrates are your body's primary and preferred energy source. They include:
- **Starches** (bread, buns, fries, rice, pasta) — complex carbs broken down slowly
- **Sugars** (sodas, sauces, ketchup, buns) — simple carbs digested quickly
- **Fibre** (vegetables, whole grains, legumes) — indigestible carbs (tracked separately below)

**Why it matters:** Carbs aren't inherently bad — your brain runs almost exclusively on glucose (a carb), and your muscles rely on glycogen (stored carbs) for intense exercise. The issue with fast food carbs is the *type*: most fast food carbs are refined (white flour buns, white potato fries) with minimal fibre. These cause rapid blood sugar spikes followed by crashes, which leave you hungry and low-energy sooner.

**In fast food:** Fast food is very carb-heavy — buns, fries, tortillas, and rice are all high-carb. A single Crunchwrap Supreme has 71g of carbs.

**Daily goal:** ~275g per day

---

### 🥑 Total Fat (g)

**What it is:** Fat is a macronutrient with several critical functions: absorbing fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), producing hormones, protecting organs, maintaining cell membranes, and providing a concentrated energy reserve.

**Why it matters:** Fat is calorie-dense — 1 gram of fat contains 9 calories, compared to 4 calories per gram for protein or carbs. This is why fatty foods are high in calories relative to their volume. Not all fats are equal: unsaturated fats (in nuts, avocados, olive oil, fish) are beneficial; trans fats and excessive saturated fats are harmful.

**In fast food:** Most fast food is high in fat due to frying oil, beef fat, cheese, and mayo. A Double Whopper has 57g of fat — nearly an entire day's worth.

**Daily goal:** ~78g per day

---

### 🧈 Saturated Fat (g)

**What it is:** Saturated fat is a specific type of fat found primarily in animal products (beef, pork, full-fat dairy, cheese, cream) and some plant oils (coconut oil, palm oil). It's "saturated" with hydrogen atoms, making it solid at room temperature.

**Why it matters:** High saturated fat intake raises LDL ("bad") cholesterol in the blood, which is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease (heart attack, stroke). Health Canada, the Heart and Stroke Foundation, and the American Heart Association all recommend limiting saturated fat to less than 10% of total daily calories.

**In fast food:** Saturated fat is fast food's most dangerous nutritional concern for long-term health. A single Baconator has 26g — more than a full day's recommended limit. Cheese, beef patties, and cream-based sauces are the biggest sources.

**Daily goal:** Less than 20g per day (< 10% of 2,000 calories)

**Fast food context:**
- Wendy's Baconator: 26g sat fat ❌ (130% of daily limit)
- Chipotle Sofritas Bowl: 5g sat fat ✅
- Starbucks Frappuccino (Grande): 12g sat fat

---

### 🧂 Sodium (mg)

**What it is:** Sodium is the mineral component of table salt (sodium chloride). It plays essential roles in fluid balance, nerve transmission, and muscle function. However, the body needs only a very small amount — about 500mg per day — to function normally.

**Why it matters:** The vast majority of Canadians and Americans consume far more sodium than they need. Chronic high sodium intake is the leading dietary cause of high blood pressure (hypertension), which in turn drives heart disease, stroke, and kidney disease. Fast food is by far the biggest source of dietary sodium in the modern diet.

**In fast food:** Fast food sodium levels are staggering. A single KFC Famous Bowl has 2,090mg — nearly a full day's intake. A Subway Footlong ham sub has 2,330mg — exceeding it. Even "healthier" items like salads often have 800–1,200mg from dressing.

**Daily goal:** Less than 2,300mg per day (Health Canada, WHO)

**Fast food context:**
- KFC Famous Bowl: 2,090mg ❌
- Subway Footlong BLT Ham: 2,330mg ❌
- McDonald's Side Salad: 10mg ✅ (dressing adds more)

---

### 🍬 Sugar (g)

**What it is:** Sugar is a simple carbohydrate. There are two types:
- **Natural sugars** — found in fruit (with fibre) and dairy (with protein). These are less concerning because they come with other nutrients that slow absorption.
- **Added sugars** — added during food processing or cooking to improve taste. This is what health guidelines refer to when they recommend limiting sugar.

**Why it matters:** Added sugars provide calories with zero nutritional value ("empty calories"). They cause rapid blood sugar spikes, promote fat storage when consumed in excess, contribute to tooth decay, and are strongly linked to obesity and type 2 diabetes. Liquid sugar (in drinks) is especially harmful because it doesn't trigger the same satiety signals as solid food.

**In fast food:** Sugar hides everywhere in fast food — in buns, ketchup, BBQ sauce, coleslaw, salad dressing, and especially in drinks. A medium Iced Capp from Tim Hortons has 43g of sugar (86% of a daily limit) in one beverage.

**Daily goal:** Less than 50g per day of added sugars

**Fast food context:**
- Tim Hortons Iced Capp (medium): 43g sugar 🚨
- Starbucks Java Chip Frappuccino (Grande): 60g sugar 🚨
- McDonald's Medium Vanilla Shake: 73g sugar 🚨
- McDonald's Side Salad: 2g sugar ✅

---

### 🌿 Fibre (g)

**What it is:** Dietary fibre is the portion of plant food that your digestive system cannot break down. It passes through your gut largely intact. There are two types:
- **Soluble fibre** (oats, beans, apples) — dissolves in water, forming a gel that slows digestion and reduces cholesterol
- **Insoluble fibre** (whole grains, vegetables) — adds bulk to stool and speeds transit through the intestine

**Why it matters:** Fibre slows digestion (keeping you full longer), feeds beneficial gut bacteria (supporting immunity and mental health), lowers LDL cholesterol, stabilises blood sugar, and reduces colorectal cancer risk. Most North Americans get far less than the recommended amount.

**In fast food:** Fast food is almost universally low in fibre because it relies on refined grains, processed meat, and cooked fats — all fibre-free. A typical fast food meal has 2–5g of fibre. The best fast food sources of fibre are beans (Chipotle, Taco Bell), vegetables, and salads.

**Daily goal:** 28g per day

**Fast food context:**
- Chipotle Sofritas Bowl: 11g ✅ (39% of daily goal)
- Wendy's Chili (Large): 8g ✅
- McDonald's Big Mac: 3g (11% of daily goal)
- KFC Original Recipe Breast: 0g

---

### 🫀 Cholesterol (mg)

**What it is:** Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance produced naturally by your liver and found in animal-based foods. It's essential — your body uses it to make cell membranes, hormones (including testosterone and estrogen), and vitamin D.

**Why it matters:** For decades, dietary cholesterol was considered a primary driver of heart disease. Current research shows that for most people, dietary cholesterol has less impact on blood cholesterol than previously thought — it's saturated fat and trans fat that have a larger effect on LDL cholesterol. However, people with diabetes or certain genetic conditions (familial hypercholesterolaemia) are more sensitive to dietary cholesterol and should still monitor it carefully.

**In fast food:** The highest cholesterol items in fast food are egg-based breakfast items (McMuffins, Croissan'wiches) and beef/cheese combinations. 

**Daily goal:** Less than 300mg per day

**Fast food context:**
- Egg McMuffin: 235mg (78% of daily limit) from the egg
- McDonald's Large Vanilla Shake: 55mg
- Grilled chicken options: typically 60–80mg

## Daily Reference Values

These values are what MacroCutie uses to calculate "% of daily goal" in your nutrition breakdown. They are based on Health Canada's Dietary Reference Intakes and the US FDA's Daily Reference Values for an average adult on a 2,000-calorie diet.

**Important:** These are population-level averages. Your personal needs will vary based on your age, sex, body weight, and activity level. A 250lb male athlete has very different needs than a 130lb sedentary female.

| Nutrient | Daily Goal | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 2,000 kcal | Health Canada / FDA |
| Protein | 50g | Health Canada DRI |
| Total Carbohydrates | 275g | FDA Daily Value |
| Total Fat | 78g | FDA Daily Value |
| Saturated Fat | < 20g | Health Canada / AHA |
| Sodium | < 2,300mg | Health Canada / WHO |
| Added Sugar | < 50g | Health Canada / FDA |
| Dietary Fibre | 28g | Health Canada DRI |
| Cholesterol | < 300mg | Health Canada |

**Reference links:**
- [Health Canada — Dietary Reference Intakes](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/nutrients.html)

## How Data is Collected

### Methodology

**1. Official sources only**
All nutritional values come directly from each restaurant's official published nutrition guide. We never estimate, calculate, or interpolate values. If a restaurant hasn't published a number, that item is not included.

**2. Canadian data priority**
For chains that operate in Canada, we use the Canadian nutrition guide as the primary source. Canadian and US menu items sometimes differ in:
- Portion sizes (grams)
- Ingredient formulations (different suppliers)
- Calorie and macro content (often slightly different)
- Menu item availability (some items are Canada-exclusive)

**3. Standard preparation assumed**
All values assume the item is prepared in the standard/default way as described on the restaurant's official menu. A Whopper's calorie count includes its standard toppings (lettuce, tomato, onion, ketchup, mayo, pickles). Modifications (remove mayo, add cheese) are supported through the condiment/modification system.

**4. Condiment brand accuracy**
Rather than using generic estimates ("ketchup = 15 cal"), MacroCutie uses the actual brand nutrition label for each condiment at the standard serving size used by restaurants. This matters: Heinz ketchup has a different calorie count than a store-brand generic.

**5. Location data methodology**
Restaurant locations (which chains operate in which cities and neighbourhoods) are determined from publicly available store locator data for each chain. **The nutrition data itself is identical across all locations of a chain** — a Big Mac has the same calories whether you're at the McDonald's in Scarborough or Vancouver. Location data helps users find which chains they have local access to, not to adjust nutrition values.

**6. Review schedule**
We aim to review all chain nutrition data at least annually. Restaurant chains do occasionally update their recipes, change ingredient suppliers, or adjust portion sizes — sometimes without announcement. If you notice a discrepancy between MacroCutie's data and what a chain currently publishes, the official source link in the Data Sources table above will always be the most current.

## Accuracy & Disclaimer

Nutrition values in MacroCutie are sourced from publicly available restaurant data and are provided **for informational and educational purposes only**. They are not medical advice.

Values may vary slightly due to:
- Regional ingredient differences (e.g., Canadian vs US beef suppliers, regional dairy)
- Preparation variation at individual restaurant locations (portion accuracy, ingredient freshness)
- Seasonal menu changes or limited-time item variations
- Restaurant recipe updates since our last data review
- Rounding differences between restaurant-reported values and regulatory reporting standards

**For allergy decisions, always verify directly with the restaurant.** Cross-contamination risks, allergen presence, and ingredient substitutions can vary by location and are not fully captured in standard published nutrition guides.

If you have a medical condition requiring precise dietary management (diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, phenylketonuria, or similar), this app should be used as a reference tool only — consult a registered dietitian for personalised guidance.

## Technical Overview

For developers or anyone curious about how MacroCutie is built:

### Stack
- **Frontend:** React 18 + TypeScript + Vite, served on port 5000
- **Backend:** Express + TypeScript API, served on port 3001
- **Database:** PostgreSQL (Replit built-in), automatically seeded on first run
- **Styling:** Tailwind CSS v3, custom design system with pastel pink/purple/mint palette
- **Font:** Nunito (Google Fonts) — rounded, friendly letterforms
- **Animation:** Framer Motion for page transitions and micro-interactions
- **Routing:** Wouter (lightweight React router)

### Database schema
- `restaurants` — 30+ chain records with metadata
- `menu_items` — 140+ items with full 9-nutrient profiles
- `condiments` — 43+ condiments with brand data
- `restaurant_condiments` — which condiments each chain offers
- `restaurant_locations` — 1,373+ city/neighbourhood coverage records

### Key API endpoints
```
GET  /api/restaurants               — list (supports city, neighborhood, search, dietary, featured filters)
GET  /api/restaurants/locations     — all cities + Toronto neighbourhoods
GET  /api/restaurants/stats         — aggregate counts
GET  /api/restaurants/:slug         — single restaurant with locations
GET  /api/menu-items                — items (supports restaurant_slug, category, dietary filters)
GET  /api/condiments                — condiments (supports category, search)
POST /api/nutrition/calculate       — full meal nutrition calculation
```

### Running locally
```bash
npm install
# Terminal 1 — backend
npx tsx server/index.ts
# Terminal 2 — frontend  
npx vite --root ./client
```

The database is automatically created and seeded (restaurants → menu items → condiments → locations) on first backend start.
